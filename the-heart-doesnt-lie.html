A self-experiment in aerobic efficiency, biomechanics, and the cost of training at 30°C — tracked to the heartbeat.

The heart does not lie. You can game pace. You can explain away a bad session. But the relationship between how fast your heart beats and how fast your legs move — that ratio, tracked over weeks — tells you something the mirror can't.

Between April 16 and June 23, 2026, I ran 17 easy sessions in Chennai — a city where the air itself feels like a second workout. I wore a Garmin, logged every FIT file, and ran the numbers through a biomechanics analysis pipeline built from scratch. What follows is what I found.

Spoiler: the news is mostly good, with one recurring flag I can't ignore.

The metric that matters: efficiency, not pace

Most runners fixate on pace. I did too — until I started logging power. The number I care about now is called the Efficiency Factor: watts produced per heartbeat. It's the Joe Friel / TrainingPeaks standard for aerobic development, and it doesn't care whether you ran 5km or 12km, whether it was cool or a furnace.

My EF moved from 1.536 W/bpm on the first run to 1.634 W/bpm on the last. That's a 6.4% gain in true aerobic output over ten weeks. For reference: below 1.4 is beginner territory; above 1.8 is trained. I'm moving through the middle of the recreational band — and moving in the right direction.

Efficiency Factor (W/bpm) across 17 easy runs

The pace barely budged — around 7.3–7.5 min/km throughout. But the heart is doing less work to produce the same output. That's adaptation. That's what easy running is supposed to build.

The flag I can't explain away

Here's the uncomfortable part. Aerobic decoupling measures how much your heart rate drifts upward relative to your pace within a single run. Under 5% is the benchmark for an aerobically fit run. My median? 7.1%. Only 3 of 17 runs came in under threshold.

Including the last two

Now, there's a major confound: 14 of my 17 runs happened at 30°C or above. Heat drives cardiac drift regardless of fitness. The question is whether the decoupling is heat-induced or fatigue-induced — and probably, it's both. The last two runs (June 16 and June 23) both sat at 8.4%, suggesting load is accumulating.

So I ran the numbers. Plotted every run's weighted average temperature against average heart rate. The result was uncomfortably clean.

Temperature vs average HR — 16 easy runs (weighted avg °C)

Easy run Fit: HR = 2.88×Temp + 61.34

Every extra degree Celsius costs roughly 2.9 beats per minute. The fit is HR = 2.88 × temp + 61.34, and the correlation is strong enough that temperature alone explains a large share of the HR variance in these sessions. This matters practically: a run at 31.5°C carries a ~7 bpm cardiac penalty compared to one at 29°C — before fatigue, fitness, or pace enter the picture at all.

The two outliers are instructive. June 23 (29.8°C, 143 bpm) sits well below the regression line — the clearest evidence of genuine aerobic adaptation in the dataset, because that HR is lower than the temperature alone would predict. April 18 (31.1°C, 155 bpm) sits well above it — the highest HR of the entire block, early in the training, when neither heat acclimatisation nor aerobic base were established.

The practical implication: comparing HR or EF across sessions without accounting for temperature is misleading. A 148 bpm run at 29°C is a fundamentally different cardiovascular effort than a 148 bpm run at 32°C. Heat-adjusted EF is the metric that matters most here — and the trend still points upward even after controlling for it.

The GDI jump is the most striking: from 21.6 in April to 47.2 in May, latest reading 64.4. My ground-contact behavior is dramatically more consistent now than when I started. This is a signal that the connective tissue and motor patterns are adapting to the volume.

One nuance the data revealed: in heat, GDI drops sharply. On cool days (n=8), GDI averaged 51.0. On hot days (n=9), it fell to 26.8. The heat doesn't just raise your heart rate — it degrades the quality of your footstrike mechanics. Something to monitor in Chennai.

The most interesting correlation in the whole dataset: GCT and pace have a near-perfect r=0.948 relationship. Ground contact time predicts pace almost exactly. Shorten your time on the ground — even slightly — and speed follows. This is where the next phase of training should focus.

The protein mass uptick — from 14.8 to 15.0 kg — mirrors the muscle mass gain and confirms it's contractile tissue being added, not water weight. The body water rise is also a positive signal: better-hydrated muscle cells are more metabolically active and recover faster. In a Chennai summer, it also means the hydration habits are working.

One honest caveat: four data points is a trend, not a verdict. Consumer bioimpedance scales have measurement variance that can swamp small week-to-week changes. What matters is the directional consistency — every metric is moving the right way across every measurement.

Sub-structure of the 101-day wellness block

Viewed as a systems timeline, these 101 days are not one smooth curve. They break into distinct physiological phases where activity load, sleep need, stress, and Body Battery signal different constraints.

Garmin stress vs body battery daily trend (Apr 16 to Jul 4)

Stress vs body battery scatter with linear fit

The relationship is strongly inverse in this window: higher daily stress is associated with lower same-day most recent Body Battery. The fitted line from your snapshot data is Body Battery = -0.43 × Stress + 41.0.

Phase map (activity + sleep + stress + body battery)

Phase 1: Baseline build (Apr 16 to May 13) Stabilizing mixed stress
Phase 2: Recovery crest (May 14 to May 23) Best recovery low RHR
Phase 3: Strain ramp (late May through June) Load accumulation stress jump
Phase 4: High-load edge (early July) Performance + strain watch fatigue

Phase 1: Baseline build. Early sessions show adaptation beginning but not yet stable. Resting HR is still relatively high, and stress has not yet separated clearly into training vs non-training drivers.

Phase 2: Recovery crest. This is the cleanest autonomic window in the dataset: low-RHR streak (10 days at 55 bpm or below), lowest daily resting HR near 44 bpm, and the strongest short HRV window (average around 62.5, peak 69). This is the reference pattern for what "absorbing training" looks like in your data.

Phase 3: Strain ramp. June stress rises sharply (43.9 monthly average vs 28.4 in May), while volume and quality work continue. The June 9 outlier (average stress 82, max 97, minimal movement) suggests at least part of the load is systemic rather than purely mechanical.

Phase 4: High-load edge. Early July combines top-end activity and top-end stress (for example July 4). This is a productive but fragile state: fitness can still move forward, but recovery margin narrows and day-to-day readiness becomes more sleep- and stress-sensitive.

In this 101-day window, the key pattern is a recovery-led peak in mid-May followed by a stress-dominant phase in June and early July. The same training load becomes more expensive when non-training stress climbs.
Operational read How to use this sub-structure

Use resting HR + sleep-need delta + average stress as a daily control stack. When all three trend adverse together, cap intensity even if pace/power still look good.

What comes next

The data points to one clear decision: a recovery week. The last two easy runs both show 8.4% decoupling. The total training volume (easy + interval + strength) has been consistently high since early May. The body has been absorbing; it needs to express.

After that, the roadmap is cleaner than ever:

Keep building EF. Another 4–6 weeks of easy volume, targeting 10–12km per run, aiming to finally push median decoupling below 5%.

Attack GCT. The r=0.948 correlation between ground contact and pace is a gift. Drills, strides, and cadence work can move this metric — and the pace will follow.

Adjust for the heat, don't fight it. I can't run easy at 7:30 min/km pace and compare it fairly to a 22°C threshold session. The training needs a heat-adjusted pacing model. The biomechanics index on hot days is an early version of that.

Keep the body composition trajectory. Fat is dropping, muscle is building, protein mass is up. Four measurements in, the trend is clean. The next goal: push body fat below 24% without sacrificing muscle — achievable at this pace over the next 8–10 weeks.

Running in this city is hard. The air resists. The sun takes. But ten weeks of data say: the heart is getting quieter for the same work. That's the whole game.
