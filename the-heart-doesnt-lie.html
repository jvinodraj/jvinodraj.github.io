Training composition: the big picture

Before breaking down the interval sessions and the strength training, here's how the whole block adds up. Strength actually outweighs the easy running in hours — so it forms the base here, not the tip.

Training pyramid — Apr 15 to Jul 18

Strength ended up carrying the largest single share of hours (40%) — a byproduct of consistency rather than intent, since each session ran close to an hour. The aerobic base and the interval/threshold/tempo work split the rest at 33% and 26%. That's a leaner base than a textbook pyramid would call for, but it lines up with a short block aimed at a specific race rather than a full aerobic-development cycle.

The metric that matters: Its efficiency, not the pace

As a runner, we have a practice of tracking the pace... even I used to be as well — until I realized the power metric that is least to be considered. The number I found more useful is Efficiency Factor: how many watts I produce for every heartbeat. One of the standard measures for tracking aerobic fitness. It works the same no matter whether the run was 5km or 12km, and whether the day was cool or very hot.

Efficiency Factor went from 1.536 W/bpm on my first run to 1.642 W/bpm on my most recent one. That's a 6.9% improvement in true aerobic fitness over 13 weeks of training. For a better understanding, a score below 1.4 usually means a beginner, and above 1.8 usually means a well-trained runner. I'm in the middle of the range for a regular recreational runner.

Efficiency Factor (W/bpm) across 21 easy runs

My pace barely changed — stayed with 7.3 to 7.5 minutes per kilometer the whole training. Over the period of time, I was able to handle the load better gradually and keep increasing week after week. Barely from 153 bpm to 145 bpm.

The problem I can't explain away

Here's the part I can't ignore. Aerobic decoupling measures how much your heart rate rises compared to your pace during a single run. A run is considered aerobically fit when this number stays under 5%. My median was 7.1%. Only 3 of my 21 runs stayed under that 5% mark.

But there's an important factor to consider: 18 of my 21 runs happened at 30°C or hotter. Heat alone pushes your heart rate up during a run, no matter how fit you are. So it's hard to know how much of my decoupling is caused by heat and how much by fatigue — it's probably both. The latest run (July 14) also came in at 8.4%, which suggests fatigue is still present.

So I compared each run's weighted average temperature against its average heart rate. The pattern was very clear.

Temperature vs average HR — 19 easy runs (weighted avg °C)

Notebook used for this analysis: easy_run_daily_hr_smoothness.ipynb.

Easy run Fit: HR = 2.88×Temp + 57.1

Each extra degree Celsius adds roughly 2.9 beats per minute to my heart rate. The formula is HR = 2.88 × temperature + 57.1, and temperature alone explains most of the difference in heart rate between my runs. In practical terms: a run at 31.5°C puts about 7 more beats per minute of strain on my heart than a run at 29°C — before fatigue, fitness, or pace are even considered.

Two runs stand out. On June 23 (29.8°C, 143 bpm), my heart rate was lower than the temperature alone would predict — the clearest sign of real fitness improvement in the whole dataset. On April 18 (31.1°C, 155 bpm), my heart rate was higher than expected — the highest of the entire ten weeks, early on, before I had adapted to the heat or built an aerobic base.

This means comparing heart rate or Efficiency Factor between runs without accounting for temperature can be misleading. A heart rate of 148 bpm at 29°C is a very different effort than 148 bpm at 32°C. Adjusting Efficiency Factor for heat is the more accurate way to track progress here — and even after making that adjustment, my trend still points upward.

Week by week: how my body adapted

Looking at weekly averages instead of single runs shows the pattern more clearly — good weeks followed by harder weeks, where the body seems to be recovering and rebuilding before the next gain shows up.

Weeks 10 and 11 slipped back toward higher fatigue, weeks 12 and 13 stabilized, and week 14 moved back into fatigue risk. A recovery week is still overdue.

Session (June 27th) was 101 minutes and 16km — the longest of the block. I completed eleven reps with almost no pace drop-off (0.15%) and very consistent power output (varying by just 1.6%). By these numbers, this was the most controlled hard session of the thirteen weeks.

The highest stress day was June 9: average stress 82 and maximum stress 97, despite very low activity (608 steps, 0.48 km). That pattern usually points to non-training load such as illness pressure, poor recovery, or life/work stressors.

An expanded pull of the stress dataset (86 daily readings, April 16 through July 10) confirms the June spike was a peak, not a new normal: July has averaged 33.5, back below June's 43.9 and closer to the April–May range. Across the full window, mean stress sits at 33.4 (median 32), with 25.6% of days landing in the high-stress band and an equal share landing low — a wide spread, but one that's trending back down. A correlation pass across wellness metrics reinforces the earlier read: stress tracks most strongly (inversely) with Body Battery — most-recent value (r = −0.52), charged value (r = −0.51), and value at wake (r = −0.45) — and positively with minimum heart rate (r = 0.59) and resting calories from activity (r = 0.47). HRV shows a moderate inverse relationship (r = −0.39). In short, the days that spike stress are the days the body never fully recharges, not simply the days with the most training volume.

3. A very strong recovery block happened in mid-May

Lowest resting HR days clustered around May 14 to May 19, including a low of 44 bpm. HRV was available for only 11 days, but that window was relatively strong: last-night HRV averaged around 62.5 and peaked at 69. Together with the low-RHR phase, that indicates a short period of excellent autonomic recovery status.

4. Sleep-need shifts are informative even with sparse sleep-stage data

Average sleep-need delta (actual minus baseline) was only +3.1 minutes overall, but with clear spikes: +70 minutes on several days (May 10, May 11, May 18, May 19, May 21) and troughs near −50 minutes on low-need days (May 4, June 13).

5. High-load days often line up with higher stress strain — but not always

Big activity days included June 18, June 20, June 27, and July 4, with top-day volume around 18 to 19.5 km. June 20 is the clearest pairing of the two signals: high volume alongside Medium-band stress (average 65). July 4 is the useful counter-example — a refreshed pull of that day's wellness data corrects an earlier reading and shows average stress at a normal 34, not the spike originally logged. Similar training load, a much calmer physiological response — a sign that by early July, high-volume days were being absorbed rather than compounding fatigue.

Breaking the 101 days into phases

These 101 days were not one smooth trend. They break into clear phases, where training load, sleep, stress, and Body Battery each tell a different part of the story.

Garmin stress vs body battery daily trend (Apr 16 to Jul 4)

Notebook used for this analysis: garmin_stress_body_battery_trend.ipynb.

Stress vs body battery scatter with linear fit

The relationship is strongly inverse in this window: higher daily stress is associated with lower same-day most recent Body Battery. The fitted line from your snapshot data (updated after correcting the July 4 stress reading) is Body Battery = -0.56 × Stress + 44.9 — a steeper relationship than the earlier estimate, and one that lines up with the broader correlation pass (r = −0.52 between stress and most-recent Body Battery).

Phase map (activity + sleep + stress + body battery)

Phase 1: Baseline build (Apr 16 to May 13) Stabilizing mixed stress
Phase 2: Recovery crest (May 14 to May 23) Best recovery low RHR
Phase 3: Strain ramp (late May through June) Load accumulation stress jump
Phase 4: High-load edge (early July) Performance, stress easing cooling off

Phase 1: Baseline build. Early sessions show adaptation beginning but not yet stable. Resting HR is still relatively high, and stress has not yet separated clearly into training vs non-training drivers.

Phase 2: Recovery crest. This is the cleanest autonomic window in the dataset: low-RHR streak (10 days at 55 bpm or below), lowest daily resting HR near 44 bpm, and the strongest short HRV window (average around 62.5, peak 69). This is the reference pattern for what "absorbing training" looks like in your data.

Phase 3: Strain ramp. June stress rises sharply (43.9 monthly average vs 28.4 in May), while volume and quality work continue. The June 9 outlier (average stress 82, max 97, minimal movement) suggests at least part of the load is systemic rather than purely mechanical.

Phase 4: High-load edge. Early July still combines top-end training activity with real training volume, but stress itself has pulled back: a corrected reading for July 4 (34, not the originally logged 80) and a 10-day July average of 33.5 both point the same direction — the June strain phase peaked and is resolving even as the training load holds. Recovery margin is still worth watching, but the signal is now closer to "absorbing" than "compounding."

In this window, the key pattern is a recovery-led peak in mid-May, a stress-dominant phase in June, and an early-July cooldown even as training volume stays high. The same training load gets more expensive when non-training stress climbs — and cheaper again once it recedes.
Operational read How to use this sub-structure

Track resting heart rate, sleep-need change, and average stress together every day. If all three get worse at the same time, ease off training — even if pace and power still look fine.

Data quality caveats

15 days Missing wellness metrics

Apr 11–Apr 15 and Jul 11–Jul 20

The most reliable health trend window is effectively April 16 to July 4, 2026, for Body Battery, sleep, and HRV. Stress is the exception: a refreshed pull now covers 86 daily readings from April 16 through July 10, and that pass also corrected a bad reading for July 4 (originally logged as 80, actually 34) — a reminder that any single-day outlier in this dataset is worth re-checking before it drives the narrative. HRV and full sleep-stage data are only present for a short subset (mostly in May), so long-range conclusions for HRV and sleep staging should be treated as directional rather than definitive.
