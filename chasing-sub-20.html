Chasing Sub 20

From 23:51 to under 20 minutes. A real-time training log of what it takes to run 5K at 4:00/km pace.

This is a living article that will evolve as training progresses. Started August 2026. Target completion: End of 2026.

In Progress

I have run a lot of kilometres over the years.

Some runs were easy. Some were painful. Some made me feel strong. And some made me question why I was running at all.

But now I have a very specific goal.

I want to run 5K in under 20 minutes.

My current personal best is 23:51.

Going from 23:51 to something below 20 minutes is not a small improvement. It means running faster than 4:00 per kilometre for the entire 5K.

That is the goal I am chasing.

Why Sub 20?

A sub 20 minute 5K has always felt like one of those running milestones that separates running regularly from becoming a reasonably fast runner.

It is only 5 kilometres.

But running those 5 kilometres at around 4 minutes per kilometre is a completely different challenge.

My current pace tells me that I am not there yet.

So instead of asking myself, "How can I run 5K faster?"

I started asking a different question.

"What is stopping me from running 5K at 4:00/km?"

That question changed the way I started looking at training.

The Gap

The gap is almost one minute per kilometre. That is a big gap.

It cannot be solved by simply adding more hard runs.

I need better aerobic fitness.

I need better running economy.

I need stronger legs.

I need better running mechanics.

And most importantly, I need to be able to hold a fast pace when fatigue starts building.

That is what this training journey is really about.

Building the Engine

The first part of this journey is not about running every session fast.

It is about building the engine.

Easy runs are still important because they allow me to build aerobic capacity without constantly stressing the body. Longer easy runs help me spend more time running. Threshold sessions help me become comfortable running hard for longer periods. Intervals teach my body what faster running feels like. And strength training helps me produce and control force when running at speed.

Each session has a purpose.

I am trying to build all of these pieces together.

Strength Is Not Just Extra Training

One thing I have started looking at differently is strength training.

Earlier, I used to think about strength training as something separate from running. Now I see it as part of running performance.

For a faster 5K, I need strong glutes, hamstrings, calves and lower legs. I need to be able to produce force quickly. I also need to control that force when my legs are tired. That is where plyometrics and running specific strength work become important.

The goal isn't to become a bodybuilder. The goal is to become a better runner.

The Starting Point: Fitness Tests

Before starting this training journey, I wanted to understand something beyond my pace and heart rate.

How well is my body actually moving?

A faster 5K is not only about having a strong heart and strong legs. Mobility, posture and movement quality can also influence how efficiently I run.

So I started with a set of fitness tests covering lower body flexibility, upper body flexibility and posture.

Where I Need Work

The assessment showed that my biggest limitations are not simply in my legs.

Several of the mobility tests around the hips, ankle and upper body were marked as failing. I also had failing results for waist angle, shoulder position and upper back posture.

My dorsiflexion test was also marked as failing. When I measured it separately, I could currently reach the wall from about 2 inches away.

For adduction, my current measurement was about one and three-quarter hand spans from hip to heel during the stretch.

These measurements give me a starting point.

Instead of assuming that something is wrong with my running mechanics, I now have specific things that I can measure and work on.

What I Will Track

I don't want this assessment to become just another list of numbers.

I will repeat these tests during the training journey and compare the results with my running.

If my dorsiflexion improves, does my running feel different?

If my hip mobility improves, does my stride feel better?

If my posture improves, does fatigue affect my form differently?

I don't know the answers yet.

That is one of the things I want to find out during this journey.

Learning to Run Fast

Running at 4:00/km is very different from running at 5:00/km.

At slower speeds, I can settle into the run and control my breathing. At faster speeds, everything becomes more important.

Cadence. Stride. Ground contact. Breathing. Posture. Muscle fatigue.

Even small inefficiencies start becoming expensive.

That is why I don't want to chase the target pace in every workout. I want my body to gradually become capable of running at that speed.

The Garmin Data

I also want to use the data from my Garmin differently this time.

A watch can tell me my pace and heart rate. But those numbers can tell a much bigger story when looked at together.

I am interested in things like heart rate response, running power, cadence, efficiency and aerobic decoupling. For example, if I can run faster while keeping my heart rate under control, that tells me something. If my pace drops badly during the second half of a hard run, that tells me something too.

The goal is not to collect more numbers. The goal is to understand what those numbers are telling me.
